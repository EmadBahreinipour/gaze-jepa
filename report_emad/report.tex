\section{Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures}
\label{sec:jepa}

\subsection{Motivation}
\label{subsec:jepa-motivation}

A central open problem in artificial intelligence is the gap between how
efficiently humans learn about the world and the data-hungry nature of current
machine-learning systems. A human infant acquires an intuitive understanding of
physics, object permanence, and spatial relationships from a few months of
passive observation, whereas state-of-the-art models typically require millions
of labelled examples to achieve comparable competence on narrow tasks.

LeCun~(2022) argues that this gap stems from the absence of \emph{internal
world models} in mainstream architectures and proposes a blueprint for
self-supervised learning called the \textbf{Joint-Embedding Predictive
Architecture (JEPA)}. The key idea is to abandon the prediction of raw sensory
data and instead learn to \emph{predict in an abstract representation space},
thereby allowing the model to discard irrelevant detail and focus on the
semantic structure of its inputs.

\subsection{Limitations of Existing Paradigms}
\label{subsec:jepa-limitations}

JEPA is positioned as a response to three dominant self-supervised paradigms,
each of which suffers from a characteristic weakness:

\paragraph{Generative models.}
Architectures such as variational autoencoders and diffusion models are trained
to reconstruct every detail of the input signal. In the visual domain this
means predicting exact pixel values, including perceptually irrelevant
variations in texture, lighting, and noise. This forces the model to allocate
capacity to information that contributes little to downstream semantic
understanding.

\paragraph{Contrastive methods.}
Approaches such as SimCLR and CLIP learn representations by attracting
embeddings of similar inputs and repelling embeddings of dissimilar ones. While
effective, they require carefully constructed sets of \emph{negative samples}.
As the complexity of the data grows, the number of negatives needed increases
combinatorially, making the training signal noisy and expensive to produce.

\paragraph{Reinforcement learning.}
Pure trial-and-error learning is extremely sample-inefficient and, on its own,
is insufficient for acquiring the rich predictive world models that underpin
human cognition.

\subsection{Core Principle: Prediction in Representation Space}
\label{subsec:jepa-core}

The central insight of JEPA can be stated concisely: rather than mapping an
input to a reconstruction of missing data in the \emph{input} space, the model
maps both the observed and the missing portions of the input into a shared
\emph{representation} space and performs prediction there.

Formally, let $\mathbf{x}$ denote the observed portion of an input and
$\mathbf{y}$ the portion to be predicted. Two encoders map these to
representations:
%
\begin{align}
  s_x &= f_\theta(\mathbf{x}),  \label{eq:enc-ctx} \\
  s_y &= f_{\bar\theta}(\mathbf{y}),  \label{eq:enc-tgt}
\end{align}
%
where $f_\theta$ is the \emph{context encoder} (trained by gradient descent)
and $f_{\bar\theta}$ is the \emph{target encoder} (updated via an exponential
moving average of~$\theta$). A predictor network then estimates the target
representation from the context:
%
\begin{equation}
  \hat{s}_y = g_\phi(s_x), \label{eq:predictor}
\end{equation}
%
and the training objective minimises the distance between $\hat{s}_y$ and
$s_y$:
%
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = d\!\left(\hat{s}_y,\; s_y\right), \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
%
where $d(\cdot,\cdot)$ is typically the mean squared error.

Because the loss operates in representation space, the encoders are free to
discard unpredictable, low-level variation and retain only the information that
is both \emph{predictable} and \emph{useful}---a property that is impossible to
achieve when the training target is a raw pixel reconstruction.

\subsection{Preventing Representation Collapse}
\label{subsec:jepa-collapse}

A critical challenge in any joint-embedding framework is
\emph{representation collapse}: the trivial solution in which both encoders
map every input to the same constant vector, yielding zero loss but carrying
no information. Different families of methods address collapse in different
ways. Contrastive methods introduce explicit repulsion through negative
samples; redundancy-reduction methods (e.g.\ Barlow Twins) decorrelate
representation dimensions; JEPA follows the strategy pioneered by BYOL and
introduces \emph{architectural asymmetry}.

Concretely, the target encoder parameters~$\bar\theta$ are not updated by
gradient descent but by an exponential moving average (EMA) of the context
encoder parameters:
%
\begin{equation}
  \bar\theta \;\leftarrow\; \tau\,\bar\theta \;+\; (1-\tau)\,\theta,
  \label{eq:ema}
\end{equation}
%
with momentum coefficient $\tau \in [0.996,\, 0.999]$. This makes the target
encoder a slowly evolving reference: stable enough to provide a consistent
learning signal, yet non-trivial enough that the context encoder and predictor
must learn genuinely informative representations to track it.

\subsection{Toward Hierarchical World Models}
\label{subsec:jepa-hierarchy}

Beyond a single predictive module, LeCun envisions stacking multiple JEPA
layers into a hierarchy. Lower levels would capture short-term, fine-grained
predictions (edges, textures, local motion), while higher levels would operate
over longer time scales and more abstract quantities (object identities, goals,
plans). This hierarchical JEPA serves as a theoretical blueprint for autonomous
agents that learn world models capable of planning and reasoning---though
full implementations of this vision remain an open research direction.

\section{I-JEPA: Image-Based JEPA}
\label{sec:ijepa}

\subsection{From Theory to Images: Overview}
\label{subsec:ijepa-overview}

While Section~\ref{sec:jepa} described JEPA as a general theoretical
framework, \textbf{I-JEPA} (Image-based JEPA), introduced by
Assran et al.~(2023), is its first concrete realisation for the visual
domain. I-JEPA instantiates every component of the abstract blueprint---context
encoder, target encoder, predictor, and masking strategy---using
Vision Transformers and a carefully designed multi-block masking scheme.
The result is a self-supervised method that learns rich, semantic image
representations \emph{without} pixel-level reconstruction and
\emph{without} hand-crafted data augmentations.

\subsection{Patch Tokenisation}
\label{subsec:ijepa-patches}

Following the Vision Transformer (ViT) paradigm, I-JEPA converts a raw image
into a sequence of discrete tokens that a Transformer can process:
%
\begin{enumerate}
  \item An input image of size $H \times W$ pixels is divided into a regular
        grid of non-overlapping patches, each of size $p \times p$ pixels
        (typically $p = 16$).
  \item Each patch is flattened and linearly projected into a $D$-dimensional
        embedding vector.
  \item Learnable positional embeddings are added so that the model retains
        information about the spatial location of each patch on the grid.
\end{enumerate}
%
For a $224 \times 224$ image with $p = 16$, this yields a
$14 \times 14 = 196$ token grid. All subsequent operations---encoding,
masking, and prediction---are defined over this grid rather than over raw
pixels.

\subsection{Multi-Block Masking Strategy}
\label{subsec:ijepa-masking}

The masking strategy is central to I-JEPA and differs markedly from
other masked-image modelling approaches such as MAE. Two complementary
sets of regions are sampled from the patch grid for each training image:

\paragraph{Target blocks.}
$N_t$ rectangular regions (typically $N_t = 4$) are sampled independently.
Each block is parameterised by a \emph{scale} $s_t$ (the fraction of
total patches it covers, uniformly sampled from $[0.15,\, 0.2]$) and an
\emph{aspect ratio} $r_t$ (sampled from $[0.75,\, 1.5]$). The resulting
blocks are spatially contiguous regions of the patch grid whose positions
are chosen at random. These blocks define the portions of the image whose
\emph{representations} the model must learn to predict.

\paragraph{Context block.}
A single large rectangular region is sampled from the grid with a
comparatively large scale. All patches that fall inside any target block
are then \emph{removed} from this region. The remaining patches constitute
the context---typically between 40\% and 60\% of the total grid. Crucially,
context and target patches \textbf{never overlap}: the model receives no
direct information about the regions it is asked to predict.

\paragraph{Why large, contiguous blocks?}
This design choice is deliberate. In MAE, individual patches are masked at
random positions across the image; because nearby visible patches are almost
always available, the decoder can reconstruct missing pixels through local
interpolation without needing a deep semantic understanding. By contrast,
I-JEPA masks entire contiguous regions and predicts their \emph{abstract
representations}. Recovering the representation of a large missing area
requires the encoder to develop a global, semantic understanding of the
scene---local texture cues are insufficient.

\subsection{Encoder Architecture}
\label{subsec:ijepa-encoders}

I-JEPA employs two Vision Transformer encoders that share the same
architecture (number of layers, embedding dimension, attention heads)
but differ in their roles and update rules:

\paragraph{Context encoder $f_\theta$.}
Receives \emph{only} the context patch tokens (with their positional
embeddings) and processes them through a standard stack of Transformer
blocks (multi-head self-attention followed by feed-forward layers).
The output is a set of representation vectors
$\{s_{x_i}\}_{i=1}^{N_c}$, one per context patch.
This encoder is trained end-to-end via gradient descent.

\paragraph{Target encoder $f_{\bar\theta}$.}
Receives \emph{only} the target patch tokens and produces a set of
target representations $\{s_{y_j}\}_{j=1}^{N_t'}$, where $N_t'$ is
the total number of patches across all target blocks. No gradient
flows through this encoder; its parameters are updated exclusively
by the exponential moving average rule defined in
Equation~\eqref{eq:ema}. As discussed in
Section~\ref{subsec:jepa-collapse}, this asymmetry is the primary
mechanism that prevents representation collapse.

\subsection{Predictor Architecture}
\label{subsec:ijepa-predictor}

The predictor $g_\phi$ is a \emph{narrow} Transformer---fewer layers and
a smaller hidden dimension than the encoders---that bridges the gap
between what the model has seen and what it must infer. Its operation
proceeds in three steps:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Input assembly.}
        The context representations $\{s_{x_i}\}$ are concatenated with a
        set of learnable \textbf{mask tokens}, one for each target patch.
        Each mask token is equipped with the \emph{positional embedding}
        of the target patch it represents. This way the predictor knows
        \emph{where} in the image it must predict, even though it has no
        content information about those locations.

  \item \textbf{Transformer processing.}
        The combined sequence of context representations and positioned
        mask tokens is processed through the predictor's Transformer
        layers. Self-attention allows each mask token to attend to all
        context representations (gathering evidence from visible regions)
        and to other mask tokens (ensuring coherence across the predicted
        region).

  \item \textbf{Prediction extraction.}
        After the final layer, only the output vectors at the mask-token
        positions are retained. These vectors
        $\{\hat{s}_{y_j}\}_{j=1}^{N_t'}$ are the predicted target
        representations.
\end{enumerate}

The predictor is kept deliberately small. A high-capacity predictor could
learn to approximate the target representations on its own, allowing the
context encoder to produce weak, uninformative features. By constraining
the predictor's capacity, I-JEPA ensures that the context encoder must
bear the primary responsibility for building rich representations---the
representations that will ultimately be used for downstream tasks.

\subsection{Training Objective}
\label{subsec:ijepa-loss}

The loss is the mean squared error between the predicted and actual
target representations, averaged over all target patches:
%
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}
  = \frac{1}{N_t'}\sum_{j=1}^{N_t'}
    \left\lVert \hat{s}_{y_j} - s_{y_j} \right\rVert_2^2.
  \label{eq:ijepa-loss}
\end{equation}
%
Gradients of $\mathcal{L}$ are back-propagated through the predictor
$g_\phi$ and the context encoder $f_\theta$; the target encoder
$f_{\bar\theta}$ is updated solely via EMA
(Equation~\ref{eq:ema}).

\subsection{Absence of Hand-Crafted Augmentations}
\label{subsec:ijepa-augmentations}

Most self-supervised visual methods rely on aggressive data
augmentations---random cropping, colour jittering, horizontal flipping,
Gaussian blurring---to create multiple views of the same image and learn
invariant representations. While effective, this approach hard-codes the
researcher's assumptions about which transformations should be
irrelevant. For example, enforcing colour invariance helps image
classification but harms any task in which colour is discriminative.

I-JEPA uses \textbf{no hand-crafted augmentations}. The only source of
variability across training iterations is the random masking itself.
Because the loss is computed in representation space, the encoders are
free to learn whichever invariances are genuinely useful for prediction,
rather than those imposed externally. Empirically, this produces
representations that transfer well across a broader range of downstream
tasks than augmentation-heavy alternatives.

\subsection{Properties of Learned Representations}
\label{subsec:ijepa-representations}

The representations learned by I-JEPA occupy a distinctive position in
the landscape of self-supervised features:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Compared with pixel-reconstruction methods (e.g.\ MAE).}
        Because MAE reconstructs raw pixels, it tends to learn low-level,
        texture-oriented features. I-JEPA, predicting in representation
        space, learns more \emph{semantic} features and consequently
        requires fewer labelled examples during fine-tuning to reach
        equivalent performance.

  \item \textbf{Compared with contrastive methods (e.g.\ DINO, iBOT).}
        Contrastive methods with heavy augmentation learn highly global,
        invariant features that excel at classification but discard
        spatial and local structure. I-JEPA retains more spatial
        information while still being semantic, which improves performance
        on spatially demanding tasks such as object detection and depth
        estimation.
\end{itemize}

\noindent In summary, I-JEPA provides a balanced trade-off: its
representations are abstract enough for high-level recognition yet
spatially detailed enough for dense prediction---a property that is
directly relevant to the gaze-modelling extension discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:saccade}.

\subsection{Relevance to Gaze Modelling}
\label{subsec:ijepa-gaze-link}

Several design elements of I-JEPA have a natural correspondence with
mechanisms of biological visual attention:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Masking as gaze selection.}
        Choosing which patches the context encoder observes is analogous
        to choosing where the eye fixates; the unobserved target blocks
        correspond to peripheral regions whose content must be inferred.

  \item \textbf{Prediction as peripheral inference.}
        The predictor's task---estimating the representation of unseen
        regions from the observed ones---mirrors the brain's ability to
        construct a coherent percept from a sparse sequence of fixations.

  \item \textbf{Positional mask tokens as spatial attention.}
        The predictor explicitly encodes \emph{where} in the image it
        must predict, providing a spatial-attention signal akin to the
        target selection that precedes a saccade.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The \textsc{SaccadeJEPA} variant examined in the next section
builds on these correspondences by replacing the random, single-shot
masking of I-JEPA with a \emph{sequential, dynamic} masking process
that more closely mirrors the temporal structure of human gaze:
saccades followed by fixations, progressively accumulating information
about a scene.

\section{SaccadeJEPA: Adaptation for Attentive Vision}
\label{sec:saccade}

The repository at
\texttt{github.com/LumenPallidium/jepa} provides, alongside a standard
I-JEPA implementation (\texttt{ViTJepa}), a variant called
\textbf{SaccadeJepa}. Importantly, SaccadeJepa is the repository author's \emph{own,
unpublished} variant---it is not a reproduction of a published method.
Architecturally, it is \emph{not} a subclass of the base JEPA hierarchy;
it is a standalone \texttt{nn.Module} with its own architecture, forward
pass, and training losses. This section presents the results of a systematic reverse
engineering of the codebase and assesses the extent to which SaccadeJepa
implements a genuine visual-attention mechanism.

\subsection{Architectural Overview}
\label{subsec:saccade-overview}

Whereas I-JEPA operates on a grid of patch tokens and learns by
predicting the representations of \emph{masked} patches, SaccadeJepa
operates on \emph{full image crops} and learns by predicting how an
image representation changes under a small spatial perturbation. The
perturbation---a random rotation and translation applied in pixel
space---serves as a minimal analogue of an involuntary eye movement
(microsaccade). The model's task is to predict the target encoder's
representation of the \emph{perturbed} view given the context encoder's
representation of the \emph{original} view, plus a description of the
applied perturbation.

The main components are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{SaccadeCropper} (\texttt{saccade.py}) --- generates two
        views of the input image: the original centre crop and a
        spatially perturbed centre crop, together with the affine
        parameters that describe the perturbation.
  \item \textbf{Context encoder} --- a ConvNeXt-Tiny backbone (from
        torchvision) that encodes View~1 (the original crop) into a
        1536-dimensional vector.
  \item \textbf{Target encoder} --- a deep copy of the context encoder,
        frozen and updated exclusively via EMA, that encodes View~2 (the
        perturbed crop).
  \item \textbf{Affine embedder} --- a NeRF-style sinusoidal positional
        encoding followed by a learned linear projection that maps the
        3-dimensional affine parameters (1~rotation $+$ 2~translations)
        to a 1536-dimensional vector.
  \item \textbf{Predictor} --- a 3-layer MLP (default) or, optionally, a
        Transformer, that takes the sum of the context representation
        and the affine embedding and produces a prediction of the target
        representation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Saccade Generation}
\label{subsec:saccade-generation}

The \texttt{SaccadeCropper} produces a perturbed view in three steps:

\paragraph{Step~1: Sample affine parameters.}
For each image in the batch a rotation angle~$\alpha$ and a 2D
translation vector~$\mathbf{t}$ are drawn independently from uniform
distributions:
%
\begin{align}
  \alpha &\sim \mathcal{U}(-\alpha_{\max},\; +\alpha_{\max}),
    \qquad \alpha_{\max} = 2^{\circ} \approx 0.035\;\text{rad},
    \label{eq:saccade-rot} \\
  \mathbf{t} &\sim \mathcal{U}(-t_{\max},\; +t_{\max})^{2},
    \qquad t_{\max} = \tfrac{\text{max\_translation}}{H_{\text{in}}},
    \label{eq:saccade-trans}
\end{align}
%
where $H_{\text{in}}$ is the full input height (default 200\,px) and
$\text{max\_translation} = \lfloor 0.9\,(H_{\text{in}} - H_{\text{crop}})
\rfloor$. With default parameters this yields a maximum translation of
roughly $\pm32\%$ of the image extent.

The sampling is \textbf{purely random}: it is not conditioned on image
content, not learned, and not guided by any saliency or attention signal.

\paragraph{Step~2: Apply the affine transform.}
An affine matrix is constructed from $\alpha$ and $\mathbf{t}$ and
applied to the full-size input image via bilinear interpolation
(\texttt{torch.nn.functional.affine\_grid} $+$
\texttt{grid\_sample}).

\paragraph{Step~3: Centre crop both views.}
Both the original and the transformed image are centre-cropped to the
target resolution ($128 \times 128$ by default). This centre crop
represents the ``fixation region''---the portion of the visual field
that is actually processed by the encoders.

\subsection{Affine Embedding}
\label{subsec:saccade-affine-embed}

The three affine parameters are embedded into the encoder's
representation space using a NeRF-inspired sinusoidal encoding. For a
scalar input $v$, the encoding at $L$ frequency levels is:
%
\begin{equation}
  \gamma(v) = \bigl[\,
    \sin(\pi\,2^{0}\,v),\;\cos(\pi\,2^{0}\,v),\;
    \dots,\;
    \sin(\pi\,2^{L-1}\,v),\;\cos(\pi\,2^{L-1}\,v)
  \,\bigr].
  \label{eq:nerf-encoding}
\end{equation}
%
The translation components use $L = 4$ (producing 16 features) and the
rotation uses $L = 10$ (producing 20 features), for a total of
36~dimensions. A learned linear layer projects this 36-dimensional
vector to the encoder's output dimension (1536), and the result is
\emph{added} element-wise to the context representation before it enters
the predictor.

This embedding serves the same functional role as the positional mask
tokens in I-JEPA: it tells the predictor \emph{how} the two views
differ spatially, so that the predictor can focus on learning the
\emph{content-dependent} change in representation.

\subsection{Forward Pass}
\label{subsec:saccade-forward}

A complete forward pass proceeds as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Generate saccade:}
        $(\mathbf{x}_1,\, \mathbf{x}_2,\, \mathbf{a})
         = \texttt{SaccadeCropper}(\mathbf{x})$,
        where $\mathbf{x}_1$ is the perturbed crop, $\mathbf{x}_2$ the
        original crop, and $\mathbf{a} = (\alpha, t_x, t_y)$.
  \item \textbf{Random view swap} (50\% probability): the two views are
        exchanged and the sign of the affine parameters is flipped,
        serving as a data-augmentation strategy.
  \item \textbf{Context encoding:}
        $s_x = f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_1)$ \quad (with gradient).
  \item \textbf{Target encoding:}
        $s_y = f_{\bar\theta}(\mathbf{x}_2)$ \quad (no gradient; EMA-updated).
  \item \textbf{Affine embedding:}
        $e_a = \texttt{affine\_embedder}(\mathbf{a})$.
  \item \textbf{Prediction:}
        $\hat{s}_y = g_\phi(s_x + e_a)$.
  \item \textbf{Optional cycle consistency:}
        $\hat{s}_x = g_\phi(\hat{s}_y + \texttt{affine\_embedder}(-\mathbf{a}))$,
        enforcing that the predictor can ``undo'' the transform.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent Exactly \textbf{one} saccade is generated per image per
forward pass. There is no mechanism for sequential fixations, no
recurrence, and no accumulation of information across multiple viewing
steps.

\subsection{Training Losses}
\label{subsec:saccade-losses}

The total training loss for SaccadeJepa comprises three (optionally
four) components:

\paragraph{Prediction loss.}
The primary objective is to minimise the Huber loss between the
predicted and actual target representations:
%
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}}
  = \texttt{HuberLoss}\!\bigl(\hat{s}_y,\; s_y\bigr).
  \label{eq:saccade-pred-loss}
\end{equation}
%
The choice of Huber loss (rather than MSE as in I-JEPA) provides
robustness to outlier representations that may arise from large spatial
perturbations.

\paragraph{Cycle-consistency loss.}
When enabled, the predictor is additionally asked to invert the
transformation by predicting the original context from the predicted
target:
%
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{cycle}}
  = \bigl\lVert \hat{s}_x - s_x \bigr\rVert_2^2,
  \qquad \text{weight } \lambda_{\text{cycle}} = 0.25.
  \label{eq:saccade-cycle-loss}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{VICReg regularisation.}
To prevent representation collapse, SaccadeJepa employs the variance
and covariance terms from VICReg:
%
\begin{align}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{var}}
    &= \frac{1}{D}\sum_{d=1}^{D}
       \max\!\bigl(0,\; \gamma - \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(s_{x}^{(d)}) + \epsilon}\bigr),
       \qquad \gamma = 4,
       \label{eq:vicreg-var} \\
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{cov}}
    &= \frac{1}{D}\sum_{i \neq j}
       \bigl[\operatorname{Cov}(s_x)\bigr]_{ij}^{2},
       \label{eq:vicreg-cov}
\end{align}
%
with weights $\lambda_{\text{var}} = 25$ and
$\lambda_{\text{cov}} = 1$. The variance term ensures that each
representation dimension maintains sufficient spread, while the
covariance term decorrelates dimensions.

\paragraph{Affine prediction loss (optional).}
An alternative training mode replaces the affine embedding with an
auxiliary head that must \emph{predict} the applied affine from the
pair of representations. This introduces cosine-distance and
magnitude-difference losses on the affine parameters.

\paragraph{Total loss.}
%
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}
  = \mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}}
  + \lambda_{\text{cycle}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{cycle}}
  + \lambda_{\text{var}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{var}}
  + \lambda_{\text{cov}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{cov}}.
  \label{eq:saccade-total-loss}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Relationship to Biological Gaze}
\label{subsec:saccade-bio}

The README of the repository motivates SaccadeJepa with the observation
that \emph{``the eyes are constantly jittering and internally the brain
predicts how this will affect the visual scene.''}  This is a reference
to fixational eye movements---involuntary, small-amplitude movements
(tremor, drift, microsaccades) that occur during nominal fixation.

The following elements of SaccadeJepa have a defensible biological
analogue:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Random spatial perturbation $\to$ fixational eye
        movements.} The uniform random rotation and translation loosely
        mirror the stochastic nature of microsaccades and ocular drift.
  \item \textbf{Predictive coding.} The core training objective---
        predicting the sensory consequences of a movement before
        executing it---is a central tenet of predictive-coding theories
        of the brain.
  \item \textbf{Two-view paradigm.} The pairing of a pre-perturbation
        and a post-perturbation view echoes the pre-saccadic and
        post-saccadic retinal images that the visual system must
        reconcile.
\end{itemize}

\noindent However, several aspects that are fundamental to biological
gaze dynamics are absent from the current implementation:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{No sequential fixations.} Only a single saccade is
        produced per image. Real visual exploration involves a sequence
        of 3--5 fixations per second, each to a new location.
  \item \textbf{No saliency-driven gaze control.} Saccade targets are
        sampled uniformly at random. Biological saccades are guided by
        both bottom-up saliency (contrast, edges, colour) and top-down
        task demands.
  \item \textbf{No foveal--peripheral distinction.} Both views are
        processed at uniform resolution. The human retina has
        dramatically higher acuity at the fovea than in the periphery.
  \item \textbf{No fixation duration.} There is no temporal dimension;
        each forward pass is instantaneous and independent.
  \item \textbf{No information accumulation.} The model discards all
        state between forward passes. Biological vision integrates
        information across multiple fixations to build a coherent scene
        representation.
  \item \textbf{No inhibition of return.} There is no mechanism to
        prevent the model from revisiting recently attended locations.
  \item \textbf{Uncalibrated saccade parameters.} The $\pm2°$ rotation
        is microsaccade-scale ($<1°$ in reality), while the $\pm32\%$
        translation is closer to a full saccade (typically $5°$--$30°$).
        These scales are not calibrated to known oculomotor statistics
        such as the main-sequence relationship between saccade amplitude
        and peak velocity.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Assessment for Gaze Simulation Reuse}
\label{subsec:saccade-reuse}

SaccadeJepa is best understood as a \textbf{self-supervised
representation-learning method} that uses spatial perturbation as its
pretext task. Its connection to biological gaze is inspirational rather
than mechanistic: it captures the idea that the brain predicts the
sensory consequences of eye movements, but it does not model the
sequential, content-driven, and temporally structured nature of real
gaze behaviour.

Nonetheless, the codebase provides a clean and modular foundation. The
pipeline \texttt{SaccadeCropper} $\to$ \texttt{Encoder} $\to$
\texttt{Predictor} could serve as the \emph{inner loop} of a gaze
simulation system, where an outer controller determines \emph{where} to
look and the inner loop handles \emph{what happens when you look there}.

Table~\ref{tab:extensions} lists the principal extensions that would be

\section{A Less Obscure Reuse of SaccadeJEPA on FIND}
\label{sec:loop}

% ----------------------------------------------------------------
% Motivation
% ----------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Motivation}
\label{subsec:loop:motivation}

The reverse engineering of Sec.~\ref{sec:saccade} identified a single
architectural gap between SaccadeJEPA and an explicit gaze model.
Every component of the upstream pipeline that handles
\emph{representation} --- the ConvNeXt-Tiny context
encoder~\cite{liu2022convnext}, the EMA-updated target
encoder~\cite{grill2020byol}, the three-layer MLP predictor, the
NeRF-encoded affine embedder, and the Huber + cycle + VICReg loss
recipe of Sec.~\ref{subsec:saccade-losses} --- is well suited to
gaze: it predicts where the fovea should land in representation
space. The piece that does \emph{not} fit is upstream's
\texttt{SaccadeCropper}, which produces a single uniformly sampled
affine perturbation per image, with no sequential structure, no
foveal targeting, and no inhibition of return.

The minimal answer to the brief's reusability question is therefore
not to redesign SaccadeJEPA but to substitute one component of it.
Section~\ref{subsec:saccade-reuse} listed the missing capabilities;
this section replaces the uniform cropper with a saliency~$\times$~IOR
sequential cropper, leaves everything else unchanged, and trains the
resulting model end-to-end on the FIND
dataset~\cite{liu2016find}. The architectural commitment is one line:
\texttt{sj.saccade\_cropper = GazeCropper(...)}. The training
commitment is the upstream loss recipe applied to the patched model
without modification.

The question this section answers is whether that minimal
substitution is enough for the predictor to learn anything. The
predictor receives the encoder's representation of the current
fixation crop together with the NeRF-encoded saliency-driven
displacement to the next fixation, and is asked to predict the
EMA target encoder's representation of the next fixation crop. There
is no human-gaze supervision and no separate fixation-policy loss:
the model only learns to anticipate next-fixation representations
under a saliency-driven sampling distribution. If the predictor's
mean-squared error against the EMA target collapses substantially
during training, the substitution is viable; if it does not, the
upstream loss recipe is incompatible with the new cropper and a
deeper architectural change would be required.

% Method — the architectural substitution
% ----------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The Architectural Substitution}
\label{subsec:loop:method}

The substitution lives in the \texttt{gazejepa.jepa\_reuse} module
and its public entry point \texttt{make\_gaze\_jepa(...)}. The factory
instantiates an unmodified \texttt{SaccadeJepa} from the upstream
repository~\cite{lumenpallidium2024jepa}, reads the prebuilt
cropper's NeRF dimensions, constructs a matching
\texttt{GazeCropper}, and replaces the upstream cropper attribute
with it. Every other module of the model --- context encoder, target
encoder, predictor, affine embedder, channel-adaptation conv, and
the loss machinery --- is reused without code changes.

\paragraph{The new cropper.}
\texttt{GazeCropper} takes a $(B, 3, H, W)$ image batch and produces
the same three-tuple $(\mathbf{view\_1}, \mathbf{view\_2},
\mathbf{affines})$ that the upstream cropper does, so the rest of
\texttt{SaccadeJepa.forward} cannot tell the two apart. Internally,
for each image the cropper executes the loop sketched in
Algorithm~\ref{alg:gaze-cropper}: a saliency map is computed once
under a static-scene assumption, an inhibition-of-return
mask~\cite{posner1984ior, klein2000ior} is initialised to all ones,
and $T$~fixations are drawn sequentially by Categorical sampling on
the elementwise product of saliency and the current IOR mask. After
each draw the IOR mask adds a Gaussian well of width
$\sigma_{\text{IOR}}{=}20$~px at the chosen location and decays
multiplicatively toward unity by a factor~$0.7$, so older
suppressions fade as new ones accumulate. The $T$ fixations are then
packed into $T-1$ consecutive transitions
$(\mathrm{fix}_t \to \mathrm{fix}_{t+1})$, each contributing a
$128 \times 128$ crop at $\mathrm{fix}_t$, a $128 \times 128$ crop
at $\mathrm{fix}_{t+1}$, and a NeRF-encoded normalised displacement
between them.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\texttt{GazeCropper.forward}: saliency~$\times$~IOR
  sequential cropping. ``$\odot$'' is elementwise product;
  $\mathrm{Cat}(\cdot)$ is a Categorical distribution over the image
  grid; $\mathrm{NeRF}(\cdot)$ is the upstream sinusoidal encoder
  with the same $(L_t, L_r)$ as \texttt{SaccadeCropper}.}
\label{alg:gaze-cropper}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Image $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times 3 \times H \times W}$;
  $T$~fixations per image; $\sigma_{\text{IOR}}$, decay $\rho$.
\For{each image $b \in \{1, \ldots, B\}$}
  \State $\mathbf{s} \gets \mathrm{saliency\_source}(\mathbf{x}_b)$
    \Comment{static-scene assumption}
  \State $\mathbf{M} \gets \mathbf{1}_{H \times W}$
    \Comment{inhibition-of-return mask}
  \For{$t = 1, \ldots, T$}
    \State $\mathrm{fix}_t \sim \mathrm{Cat}(\mathbf{s} \odot \mathbf{M} +
      \epsilon)$
    \State $\mathbf{M} \gets \rho \cdot \mathbf{M} + (1 - \rho)$
      \Comment{decay toward unity}
    \State $\mathbf{M} \gets \mathbf{M} \cdot \bigl[\mathbf{1} -
      \mathcal{N}(\mathrm{fix}_t, \sigma_{\text{IOR}})\bigr]$
      \Comment{add Gaussian well}
  \EndFor
  \For{$t = 1, \ldots, T-1$}
    \State $\mathbf{view}_1[b, t] \gets
      \mathrm{crop}_{128 \times 128}(\mathbf{x}_b, \mathrm{fix}_t)$
    \State $\mathbf{view}_2[b, t] \gets
      \mathrm{crop}_{128 \times 128}(\mathbf{x}_b, \mathrm{fix}_{t+1})$
    \State $\mathbf{d}[b, t] \gets (\mathrm{fix}_{t+1} - \mathrm{fix}_t) /
      (\tfrac{1}{2}H, \tfrac{1}{2}W)$
    \State $\mathbf{a}[b, t] \gets \mathrm{NeRF}(\mathbf{0}_{\text{rot}},
      \mathbf{d}[b, t])$
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\State \Return $(\mathbf{view}_1, \mathbf{view}_2, \mathbf{a})$ flattened
  to $B(T-1)$ rows.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Why the substitution is safe.}
The upstream \texttt{affine\_embedder} is a \texttt{Linear} layer
sized for the cropper's NeRF embedding: with translation order
$L_t{=}4$ and rotation order $L_r{=}10$ it expects
$L_t \cdot 4 + L_r \cdot 2 = 36$ features. \texttt{GazeCropper}
reads $L_t$ and $L_r$ from the prebuilt \texttt{SaccadeCropper}
inside \texttt{make\_gaze\_jepa(...)} and reuses the upstream NeRF
encoder; an assertion in the factory confirms the two
\texttt{affine\_embed\_dim} values match before the swap. Rotation
is set to zero throughout because saliency-driven gaze translates
the fovea but does not rotate the world. The encoder and target
encoder both operate on $3$-channel $128 \times 128$ crops, so the
EMA update copies parameters between modules of identical
architecture. Crucially, the integrated-controller variant explored
earlier in this project wrapped a $6$-channel
fovea\,$+$\,periphery encoder around an unchanged $3$-channel target,
which would have desynchronised the EMA path; that variant is
unrelated to the upstream pipeline and has been retired in favour of
the present fovea-only substitution.

\paragraph{Saliency as a pluggable interface.}
The saliency source is a duck-typed callable taking
$(B, 3, H, W)$ images and returning a non-negative,
sum-normalised $(B, H, W)$ map. The default in this section is the
classical Itti--Koch model~\cite{itti1998saliency}; replacing it
with a learned saliency CNN is a one-line change to the
\texttt{saliency\_source} keyword argument of
\texttt{make\_gaze\_jepa}. Figure~\ref{fig:loop:in-action} shows the
patched architecture with the substitution highlighted.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{../outputs/jepa_reuse_demo.png}
  \caption{The patched \texttt{SaccadeJepa} on a FIND frame at
    $200 \times 200$. \texttt{GazeCropper} (left) consumes the frame
    and a saliency map (Itti--Koch by default), runs $T{=}5$
    saliency~$\times$~IOR fixations, and emits four

    target encoder receives the second view of each transition; the
    affine embedder consumes the NeRF-encoded displacement. The
    predictor sums the encoded view~$1$ with the embedded affine and
    must recover the target encoder's representation of view~$2$.
    Every box except the cropper is upstream code, used unmodified.}
  \label{fig:loop:in-action}

\subsection{Training on FIND}
\label{subsec:loop:evaluation}

Training uses the upstream loss recipe (Sec.~\ref{subsec:saccade-losses}):
the predictor's output is compared against the EMA-target-encoder's
representation of the next-fixation crop with a Huber
loss~\cite{huber1964robust}, a cycle-consistency term reuses the
predictor with reversed views, and the VICReg
regulariser~\cite{bardes2022vicreg} drives variance and
decorrelation across the batch dimension. The objective and its
weights are vendored thinly in
\texttt{scripts/train\_jepa\_reuse.py} so the project does not depend
on the upstream training script's ImageNet imports; the function
itself is a faithful copy of the upstream \texttt{saccade\_loss}.

\paragraph{Optimisation.}
AdamW with learning rate $5 \cdot 10^{-5}$, weight decay $10^{-6}$,
$\beta_1{=}0.9$, $\beta_2{=}0.99$. The learning rate follows a
linear warm-up over the first $10\%$~of iterations, then a cosine
schedule down to $\eta_{\min}{=}10^{-5}$, exactly as in the upstream
\texttt{WarmUpScheduler}. After every optimiser step the target
encoder is updated by the standard exponential moving average with
decay $\tau{=}0.996$~\cite{grill2020byol}. Gradients are clipped at
norm~$1$.

\paragraph{Data and run shape.}
The FIND $50/6/6$ train/val/test split is shared with the saliency
study of Arash~Khosropour. Frames are decoded from the raw MP4s in
\texttt{Our\_database/raw\_videos/}, resized to $200 \times 200$ for
\texttt{GazeCropper} (the upstream default \texttt{full\_input\_size}),
and the cropper produces $128 \times 128$ fixation crops as its
output. The demonstration run uses three epochs, batch size four,
and four sampled frames per training video (i.e.~$50 \times 4 = 200$
training items per epoch), giving a CPU wall-clock of roughly
$13$~minutes per epoch. The model has $33{,}779{,}808$~trainable
parameters; with $T{=}5$~fixations per frame the predictor sees
$4 \cdot 4 = 16$~transitions per batch.

\paragraph{Hyperparameter scaling.}
The upstream VICReg defaults
($\lambda_{\mathrm{var}}{=}25$, $\lambda_{\mathrm{cov}}{=}1$,
$\gamma{=}4$) were tuned for an effective batch of
$\sim\!1024$~samples on GPU. Our CPU budget gives an effective batch
of $16$, and the variance penalty grows as the inverse standard
deviation across that batch, so the upstream weights would
overwhelm the prediction term at our scale. The training script
therefore uses $\lambda_{\mathrm{var}}{=}1.0$,
$\lambda_{\mathrm{cov}}{=}0.1$, and $\gamma{=}1.0$. These are the
only deviations from the upstream recipe; everything else (cycle
weight, loss-function shape, EMA decay, NeRF orders) is upstream.

\paragraph{Loss curve.}
Figure~\ref{fig:loop:loss-curve} plots the running loss and the
prediction-MSE component over the three-epoch run. The total loss
descends smoothly from~$\sim\!1.26$ to~$\sim\!0.79$. The prediction
MSE, which is the most directly interpretable signal, falls from
$\sim\!0.31$ at initialisation to a band around $0.02$--$0.05$
during the third epoch, with brief spikes correlated with frames
where the saliency map is unusually concentrated and the predictor
sees a near-singular displacement distribution.

\subsection{Results}
\label{subsec:loop:results}

The headline measurement is whether the predictor learns to
anticipate the EMA target encoder's representation of the next
fixation. Two views of that question are reported here: a
single-frame before/after comparison that isolates the effect of
training, and the validation-set prediction MSE recorded after every
epoch.

\paragraph{Single-frame before/after.}
At initialisation the model is a randomly weighted instance of the
upstream \texttt{SaccadeJepa} with \texttt{GazeCropper} swapped in
and no further tuning. Running the untrained model on FIND
video~$002$ frame~$100$ with $T{=}5$ fixations gives a prediction
MSE of $0.3128$ averaged across the four resulting transitions; the
predictor's $1{,}536$-dimensional output is essentially uncorrelated
with the EMA target. After the three-epoch training run, the same
forward pass on the same frame --- with the identical seed and
identical \texttt{GazeCropper} configuration --- gives a prediction
MSE of $0.0234$, a $92.5\%$ reduction. The reduction is the
substitution's primary feasibility evidence: with no architectural
surgery beyond the cropper swap, the upstream loss machinery does
drive the predictor toward the EMA target.

\paragraph{Validation-set prediction MSE.}
Table~\ref{tab:loop:ks} reports the prediction MSE measured on the
six held-out validation videos at the end of each training epoch.
The error descends monotonically and rapidly: an order of
magnitude during the first epoch alone, with a further factor of
two by epoch~$3$. The validation curve tracks the training curve
closely (training prediction MSE at the end of epoch~$3$ is
$\approx\!0.038$); there is no widening gap that would indicate
overfitting on the small CPU run. Compared with the per-frame
untrained baseline of $0.3128$, the final validation MSE of
$0.0435$ is an $86\%$ reduction; compared with the first-epoch
validation MSE of $0.0955$, it is a $54\%$ further drop.

\subsection{Discussion}
\label{subsec:loop:discussion}

The numerical findings of Sec.~\ref{subsec:loop:results} address the
brief's reusability question on three counts.

First, the substitution is genuinely minimal. The architectural
commitment is one line: \texttt{sj.saccade\_cropper = GazeCropper(...)}.
The encoder, target encoder, predictor, affine embedder, channel
adapter, and the entire loss machinery of
Sec.~\ref{subsec:saccade-losses} are reused without code changes.
The only deviation from the upstream training recipe is the
batch-size-aware rescaling of the VICReg weights described in
Sec.~\ref{subsec:loop:evaluation}; that is a numerical
accommodation, not an architectural one.

Second, the dimensional compatibility between the new and old
croppers is by construction, not by engineering after the fact.
\texttt{GazeCropper} reads $L_t$ and $L_r$ from the prebuilt
\texttt{SaccadeCropper} inside \texttt{make\_gaze\_jepa(...)} and
reuses the upstream NeRF encoder verbatim, so its
\texttt{affine\_embed\_dim} matches the prebuilt
\texttt{affine\_embedder} layer's input width regardless of how
those orders are configured. The compatibility is enforced by an
assertion in the factory and would be caught as a build-time error
if it ever broke. This is the practical value of the upstream design
choice to expose the cropper as a swappable
\texttt{torch.nn.Module}: the dimensionality contract travels with
the swap.

Third, the upstream loss recipe, applied to the patched model on
FIND, drives the predictor MSE down from $0.3128$ at initialisation
to $0.0234$ on the demonstration frame and to $0.0435$ on the
validation pool --- reductions of $92.5\%$ and $86\%$ respectively.
The quantity that descends is exactly the JEPA quantity: the
mean-squared error between the predictor's output and the EMA
target encoder's representation of an unobserved fixation crop. The
gradient signal that drives this descent comes from a self-supervised
objective with no human-gaze labels and no scanpath-similarity
reward; the only training-time information about gaze is the
saliency-driven sampling distribution that produces the
displacement~$\mathbf{d}$. This is the JEPA principle ---
prediction-in-representation-space --- transferred from random crops
to gaze transitions.

The result also clarifies a concern raised earlier in this
project. An integrated-controller variant explored before the
present design wrapped a $6$-channel fovea\,$+$\,periphery encoder
around an otherwise unchanged \texttt{SaccadeJepa}, which created an
EMA channel mismatch between the $6$-channel context branch and the
$3$-channel target branch. The current substitution uses
fovea-only $3$-channel crops, so the EMA path is symmetric: the
target encoder is a parameter-by-parameter EMA copy of the context
encoder, exactly as the upstream design intended. The earlier
mismatch was a property of that integrated-controller stack, not of
the upstream pipeline, and is not present here.

What this section does \emph{not} claim is that the trained model
matches human scanpaths. The training objective contains no
human-gaze term, and three epochs on CPU is feasibility evidence,
not a tuned model. A learned policy whose fixation choices receive
gradient through the next-fixation decision (compare
Mnih~et~al.~\cite{mnih2014ram}), a learned saliency source in place
of Itti--Koch (compare Arash's saliency comparison study), and a
longer training run on GPU would each tighten that gap. The
present section establishes only the prerequisite: the upstream
\texttt{SaccadeJepa} can be reused, with one substitution, to
operate on saliency-driven fixation transitions, and its loss
machinery converges under that substitution.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:loop:limitations}

\textbf{Static-frame assumption.} Each training and evaluation frame
is treated as a $200 \times 200$ RGB image, and the
saliency~$\times$~IOR loop runs $T{=}5$ fixations over the frozen
frame. FIND is a $30$~fps video dataset, and a saccade-aware
controller would consume short temporal windows rather than a single
frame. Concatenating two or three temporally adjacent frames as
extra input channels is a natural extension; the architectural cost
is widening the upstream channel-adaptation conv, which is its own
small piece of work and is out of scope here.

\textbf{Fixed $T{=}5$, no fixation duration.} The number of
fixations per frame is a hyperparameter, and the time spent at each
fixation is not modelled at all. FIND ships per-fixation duration
data, but it is keyed in a way that the project's data loader cannot
currently align with frame indices (see CLAUDE.md, ``Known issues''),
so duration was deliberately scoped out of the model.

\textbf{Three epochs on CPU.} The demonstration run is a feasibility
check, not a tuned model. A longer GPU run with a larger batch (so
the upstream VICReg weights of $25/1/4$ become appropriate again) is
expected to push the validation prediction MSE substantially below
$0.0435$. The ranking of saliency sources, the IOR scale, and any
choice of $T$ should be re-verified at that scale.

\textbf{Itti--Koch is a compact reimplementation.} The default
saliency source uses an intensity channel, two colour-opponent
channels with the standard Itti normalisation that suppresses pixels
below $10\%$ of peak intensity, and a Sobel-gradient magnitude in
place of the original four-Gabor orientation bank. Centre--surround
is restricted to three pairs $(c, s) \in \{(0, 2), (1, 3), (2, 4)\}$,
and the across-scale normalisation is the simplified
$N(\cdot)$-style global-mean weighting rather than the iterative
DoG-based local-maxima version of the original
paper~\cite{itti1998saliency}. The deviations are documented at the
head of \texttt{gazejepa/saliency/itti\_koch.py}.

\textbf{No learned saliency in this section.} \texttt{GazeCropper}
accepts any callable satisfying the
\texttt{gazejepa.saliency.SaliencySource} interface, and Arash's
learned-CNN saliency source is a one-line swap into
\texttt{make\_gaze\_jepa}. Substituting it and re-running the
training would test whether a data-driven saliency source produces a
predictor with lower validation MSE; doing so depends on Arash's
checkpoint and is therefore reported in the parallel saliency study
rather than here.

\textbf{No human-scanpath comparison in this section.} The headline
numbers in Sec.~\ref{subsec:loop:results} measure prediction MSE
against the EMA target, not similarity to human gaze. Saccade-amplitude
KS tests, MultiMatch position similarity, and normalised Fr\'echet
distance against the FIND human pool are produced by retargeting
\texttt{gazejepa.evaluation.evaluate.evaluate\_gaze\_loop} at the
trained model and are reported separately. The decision to keep them
out of this section is editorial: the question this section answers
is whether the upstream pipeline accepts the substitution and trains
under it, and the prediction-MSE evidence answers that question on
its own.
